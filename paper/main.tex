\begin{abstract}
Retail portfolio tools present recommendations but rarely expose the cost
structure or the data provenance behind them. We study the narrow but universal
decision of deploying incremental cash into an existing equity portfolio, and
show that when the broker fee is a step function of order size, the deployment
problem is governed by a \emph{subadditive cost}. We prove that splitting a fixed
budget can never reduce fees and we derive the economic order floor in closed
form as a fixed point of the fee-impact constraint. Treating the recommendation
engine as a pure, deterministic function lets us run exhaustive, reproducible
parameter sweeps; these sweeps surfaced a latent fee defect in our own planner
(a one-tranche overpayment for sub-tranche cash) which we characterize, fix, and
guard with a property test. A weight-ablation experiment and a real
2021--2026 backtest of a 17-name basket against four baselines---including
minimum-variance and risk-parity allocators---converge on the same humbling
observation: the fundamentals-weighted basket ranks third of five on
risk-adjusted return, behind two allocators that use no fundamental data,
echoing classical results on $1/N$ diversification \cite{demiguel2009naive}. Our
principal contribution is therefore methodological: a decision engine whose cost
behavior is provable and whose every claim is a reproducible artifact, with
empirical findings that emerged from observation rather than assumption.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{computational finance, portfolio analytics, reproducibility,

\section{Introduction}

A self-directed investor who has decided \emph{what} to own still faces a
recurring micro-decision: given a small amount of incremental cash, \emph{where}
should it go, and is the trade even worth its fee? Consumer tools answer the
first question and ignore the second. We take the second question seriously and
find that it has more structure than it appears.

We build on established quality and profitability signals
\cite{novyMarx2013, asness2019qmj}, but our contribution is not a new factor. It
is a stance: the deployment engine should be a \emph{pure, deterministic
function} of its inputs, so that its cost behavior can be reasoned about and its
outputs can be replayed, diffed, and falsified. Adopting this stance let us
treat the engine itself as an object of experiment. Three findings followed, two
of which we did not anticipate.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The broker fee is subadditive, so any policy that splits cash for
  diversification pays a quantifiable premium (Section~\ref{sec:fees}). The same
  analysis exposed a defect in our own planner.
  \item The economic order floor is not a tuned constant; it is the fixed point
  of the fee-impact constraint (Section~\ref{sec:guardrail}).
  \item On both a controlled ablation and a real backtest, sophisticated factor
  weighting is dominated by equal weighting (Section~\ref{sec:weighting}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{System Design}

The backend separates API DTOs, portfolio importers, provider adapters,
analytics scoring, and deployment planning. Network access is isolated behind
provider abstractions. The scoring and deployment modules perform no I/O: a
recommendation is a pure function of the tuple
\[
  (\text{holdings},\ \text{fundamentals},\ \text{cash},\ \text{policy version}).
\]
Referential transparency is what makes the rest of this paper possible. Every
figure and table below is produced by a single offline driver
(\texttt{scripts/run\_experiments.py}) and is regression-tested; no result
depends on live data or hidden state.

The composite score is a linear functional of three bounded sub-scores,
\begin{equation}
  s = w_m\,\mathrm{moat} + w_c\,\mathrm{comp} + w_v\,\mathrm{val},
  \qquad \mathrm{moat},\mathrm{comp},\mathrm{val}\in[0,100],
  \label{eq:composite}
\end{equation}
with $(w_m,w_c,w_v)=(0.40,0.35,0.25)$. Holdings already above $1.3\times$ equal
weight, or in a theme at its concentration cap, are excluded; the planner then
ranks the remainder by $s$ and sizes orders against available cash.

\section{The Fee Structure as an Object of Study}
\label{sec:fees}

The broker charges per started tranche of an order:
\begin{equation}
  f(a) = \left\lceil \tfrac{a}{T} \right\rceil c,
  \qquad T = \$1000,\ c = \$2.50.
  \label{eq:fee}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Subadditivity]
\label{prop:sub}
For all $a,b \ge 0$, $f(a+b) \le f(a) + f(b)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\lceil x+y\rceil \le \lceil x\rceil + \lceil y\rceil$ for all reals, and
multiplying by $c>0$ preserves the inequality with $x=a/T$, $y=b/T$.
\end{proof}

The consequence is immediate and practical: \emph{consolidating cash into one
order weakly minimizes fees}. Any split into orders $a_1,\dots,a_k$ pays a
\emph{diversification fee premium}
\begin{equation}
  \pi(a_1,\dots,a_k) = \sum_i f(a_i) - f\!\Big(\sum_i a_i\Big) \;\ge\; 0.
  \label{eq:premium}
\end{equation}

We swept cash from \$250 to \$5000 and compared three policies against the
single-order lower bound: a \emph{naive} split strictly proportional to score, a
\emph{fee-aware} planner, and (as a reference) the single order. Naive splitting
incurs $\pi=\$2.50$ in recurring bands wherever the two legs cross an extra
tranche boundary (Figure~\ref{fig:premium}).

\paragraph{A defect surfaced by the sweep.}
The sweep did not merely confirm theory; it falsified an assumption in our own
code. The planner's prior implementation aligned one leg to a whole tranche to
preserve the single-order fee, which is correct for cash above one tranche but
\emph{not} for sub-tranche cash. For cash in roughly $[\$625,\$1000]$ the planner
split into two sub-tranche orders, paying two tranches (\$5.00) where one order
costs one (\$2.50). The lower edge of the band is $\,\mathrm{MIN}/(1-\rho)\,$,
where $\rho$ is the larger score share and $\mathrm{MIN}$ the order floor of
Section~\ref{sec:guardrail}: it is the cash level at which the smaller leg first
clears the floor. We now diversify \emph{only when it is fee-neutral}, otherwise
consolidate. A property test asserts $\,\text{planner fee} \le f(\text{cash})\,$
across the grid, and the engine premium is now zero everywhere it deploys
(Figure~\ref{fig:premium}, green).

\section{The Guardrail Is a Fixed Point}
\label{sec:guardrail}

A trade should not lose a meaningful fraction of its value to fees. Fixing a
tolerance $\tau$ on fee impact for a single-tranche order gives
$f_{\min}/a \le \tau$, i.e. $c/a \le \tau$. The binding case yields the economic
order floor
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MIN} = \frac{c}{\tau} = \frac{\$2.50}{0.01} = \$250,
  \label{eq:min}
\end{equation}
the cash level at which one tranche's fee is exactly $\tau=1\%$ of the order.
This is a \emph{derived fixed point of the cost model}, not a tuned parameter.
Below \$250 the planner holds cash; a second economic order requires
$2\,\mathrm{MIN}=\$500$. Figure~\ref{fig:guardrail} shows fee impact touching
exactly $1\%$ at the floor and declining thereafter, with sawtooths only at the
genuine tranche boundaries.

\section{What Actually Drives the Decision}
\label{sec:weighting}

Because the composite (\ref{eq:composite}) is linear, the ranking it induces is
directly ablatable: zeroing a weight isolates a factor, and the score can be
recomposed from per-position sub-scores without re-fetching data.

\paragraph{Ablation (mechanism).}
On a fixed, deterministic basket we recomposed the ranking under single-factor
and equal weights and measured Spearman correlation to the baseline ordering and
the churn in the funded top-2 (Table~\ref{tab:ablation},
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}). Single factors reorder the basket sharply---moat-only
and valuation-only are \emph{anti-correlated} with the baseline---while equal
thirds reproduce the baseline almost exactly ($\rho=0.97$, zero churn). The
funded picks are stable under $\pm10\%$ weight perturbation. (This basket uses
deterministic synthetic fundamentals; it demonstrates the \emph{mechanism}, not a
claim about specific securities.)

\paragraph{Backtest against allocator baselines (real data).}
We backtested the 17-name basket against four baselines over 2021-01-05 to
2026-05-28 (1355 trading days) using free adjusted closes
(Table~\ref{tab:backtest}, Figure~\ref{fig:backtest}). Two are naive
(market-value ``current'' weighting and equal weighting); two are allocator
baselines computable from the return matrix alone---long-only minimum variance
and equal-risk-contribution (risk parity)---so no baseline uses information the
others lack. The score-weighted basket beats the SPY benchmark by a wide margin
($+4.80$ pp CAGR, $+0.15$ Sharpe) and beats minimum variance on return
($+4.16$ pp CAGR), but it ranks \emph{third of five} on the Sharpe ratio: equal
weighting ($1.19$) and risk parity ($1.18$) both edge out the tuned weighting
($1.10$), and minimum variance delivers the shallowest drawdown ($-19.1\%$ versus
$-28.9\%$) at the cost of return. Periodic rebalancing \emph{reduced} CAGR to
$18.3\%$ from the $20.6\%$ buy-and-hold figure despite low explicit cost. The
synthetic ablation and the real backtest point the same way: on this basket, two
allocators that use \emph{no} fundamental data match or beat the
fundamentals-weighted strategy on risk-adjusted return.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Principal contribution.}
We set out to let the contribution emerge from observation. It did, and it is
methodological rather than a performance claim. The defensible, novel core is the
treatment of cash deployment as deterministic optimization under a subadditive
cost: Propositions and Equations (\ref{eq:fee})--(\ref{eq:min}) are provable, and
the engine is held to them by tests. The empirical sections are evidence
\emph{for the method}, including evidence against our own initial design.

\paragraph{Takeaways and a useful negative result.}
First, in fee-quantized markets, diversification is not free; the right default
is to diversify only when fee-neutral. Second, the cost guardrail is derivable,
not tunable. Third---the result we did not expect---the fundamentals-weighted
basket ranks third of five on risk-adjusted return, behind both equal weighting
and risk parity, two allocators that consume no fundamental data. This is
consistent with $1/N$ diversification being hard to beat \cite{demiguel2009naive}.
Selection appears to matter more than weighting here; we would rather report this
than hide it. The result also clarifies where the contribution does \emph{not}
lie: not in outperforming standard allocators, but in making each deployment
decision cheap, bounded, and auditable.

\paragraph{Serendipity of measurement.}
Each finding came from instrumenting a deterministic engine rather than from a
prior hypothesis. The fee defect was invisible in code review and obvious in a
parameter sweep; the band's lower edge turned out to depend on the score split,
not a constant. Reproducibility was not a compliance checkbox but the instrument
that produced the science.

\section{Limitations}

The backtest is a current-basket study on free adjusted closes; it is subject to
survivorship and selection bias and is \emph{not} a point-in-time
stock-selection backtest. The ablation uses deterministic synthetic fundamentals
and demonstrates mechanism, not security-level claims. Out-of-sample
point-in-time evaluation, transaction-cost and tax modeling beyond the explicit
fee, and threshold-sensitivity studies remain future work. ArenaWealth Pro is
not a registered investment adviser, broker-dealer, or execution system;
commercial use requires legal, compliance, data-licensing, and suitability
review.

\section{Conclusion}

Taking determinism seriously turned a consumer recommendation engine into an
analyzable object. The fee structure is provably subadditive, the order floor is
a fixed point, and exhaustive reproducible sweeps both proved guardrails and
caught a real defect. The empirical results are deliberately modest and partly
self-critical---equal weighting is hard to beat---because they were observed, not
assumed. We argue this is the right posture for computational-finance artifacts:
make every claim reproducible, and let measurement, not narrative, decide what
the contribution is.
