\begin{abstract}
Large language models increasingly generate investment recommendations, and a
growing literature evaluates them by return or by agreement with a reference
portfolio. We argue that agreement is the wrong yardstick and provide a better
one: a \emph{deterministic, replayable baseline}---a quality, moat, and
compounding policy with provable cost guardrails---together with an audit
protocol that scores any advisor on three independent axes: operational
\emph{validity} under explicit constraints, \emph{stability} across repeated
runs, and \emph{agreement} with the baseline. On a set of frozen scenarios we
show the axes are independent: a control that agrees perfectly with the policy
and is perfectly stable is nonetheless \emph{invalid} because it wastes fees,
while a valid control is unstable. An evaluation that reports only
recommendation overlap would rank both as good advice. The baseline earns its
role as a yardstick through reproducibility: its cost behavior is provable (the
fee is subadditive and the economic order floor is a closed-form fixed point),
its decisions are pure functions of their inputs, and a 2021--2026 backtest
against minimum-variance, risk-parity, and equal-weight allocators shows the
baseline does not overclaim---equal weighting matches it, consistent with the
difficulty of beating $1/N$ \cite{demiguel2009naive}. The artifact regenerates
every figure offline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models now produce investment recommendations, and recent work
evaluates them by realized return or by agreement with a reference portfolio
\cite{oh2025alpha, spadea2025flarko}. Both yardsticks are unsafe. Return is
noisy and invites overfitting; agreement rewards an advisor for matching a
reference even when its recommendation is operationally unusable or unstable
across runs. Parallel evidence shows learned advisors are inconsistent under
irrelevant changes and carry latent bias \cite{chawla2025riskadvice, lee2025bias},
which a return or overlap score cannot detect.

We take a different stance: evaluate an advisor against a \emph{deterministic,
replayable baseline} on three axes that a single number conflates---operational
\emph{validity} under explicit constraints, \emph{stability} across repeated
runs, and \emph{agreement} with the baseline. The baseline is a transparent
quality, moat, and compounding policy whose recommendations are pure functions
of their inputs, so any run can be replayed and diffed. Its trustworthiness as a
yardstick rests on reproducibility, not on a claim to beat the market.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An audit protocol and a frozen-scenario benchmark that score any advisor
  on validity, stability, and agreement, and a finding that these axes are
  \emph{independent}: a control can agree perfectly and be perfectly stable yet
  be invalid, so agreement-only evaluation is misleading (Section~\ref{sec:audit}).
  \item The deterministic baseline that makes the protocol trustworthy: its fee
  is provably subadditive and its economic order floor is a closed-form fixed
  point (Sections~\ref{sec:fees}--\ref{sec:guardrail}); exhaustive reproducible
  sweeps even surfaced and fixed a latent defect in our own planner.
  \item An honest calibration of the baseline: a 2021--2026 backtest against
  minimum-variance, risk-parity, and equal-weight allocators shows it does not
  overclaim---equal weighting matches it on risk-adjusted return
  (Section~\ref{sec:weighting}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Our work sits at the intersection of four lines.

\paragraph{What to own: quality and profitability.}
Factor research establishes durable signals for security selection: gross
profitability \cite{novyMarx2013} and the quality-minus-junk composite
\cite{asness2019qmj}. We treat selection as given and do not propose a new
factor; our scoring reuses these signals only to rank an existing basket. The
contribution is orthogonal---the layer that decides \emph{how to deploy cash}
into an already-chosen basket, net of frictions.

\paragraph{How to trade it: transaction-cost theory and planning.}
Classical theory shows that transaction costs induce a \emph{no-trade region}:
proportional costs make the optimal policy a band around the target where one
does not rebalance \cite{constantinides1986capital, davis1990portfolio}. Fixed
plus linear costs turn allocation into a mixed-integer or convex program
\cite{lobo2007portfolio}, and richer institutional models add liquidity and
market-impact terms \cite{zhang2019dynamic}. On the operational side, rebalancing
under fixed per-transaction fees can be cast as a planning problem solved by
linear programming or heuristic search \cite{delarosa2023planning}. We differ on
three axes. First, our fee is \emph{quantized}---a step function in order
size---so the object of study is its subadditivity, not a smooth or purely linear
cost. Second, we analyze the recurring \emph{incremental} deployment of small
cash rather than a one-shot rebalance or a full trade plan. Third, we prioritize
closed-form, auditable guardrails over numerical optimality, because a decision a
retail investor cannot inspect is a decision they cannot trust. The fixed-cost
guardrail is the small-cash analogue of the no-trade region: below the floor, the
optimal action is to hold.

\paragraph{Evaluating learned investment advice.}
A fast-growing line uses language models to recommend assets or build portfolios
and evaluates them mainly by return or by alignment with a reference: media-driven
LLM portfolios report strong but short-horizon gains \cite{oh2025alpha}, and
knowledge-graph-aligned recommenders are scored on behavioral alignment
\cite{spadea2025flarko}. A complementary line documents failure modes a return or
overlap score cannot see: LLM risk assessments vary with irrelevant demographic
attributes \cite{chawla2025riskadvice}, and investment analyses carry latent,
confirmation-seeking bias \cite{lee2025bias}; surveys flag interpretability and
generalizability as open problems \cite{elalami2025mlfinance}. What is missing is
a single evaluation that places a learned advisor against a transparent,
replayable baseline and measures, together, whether its advice is operationally
\emph{valid}, \emph{stable} across runs, and how far it \emph{agrees} with the
baseline. We supply that baseline and protocol; the advisor under test may be an
LLM, a robo-allocator, or a heuristic.

\paragraph{Whether to believe it: reproducibility over overfitting.}
A large fraction of reported strategy performance does not survive out of
sample; backtest overfitting is easy to produce and hard to detect
\cite{bailey2014pseudo}, and naive $1/N$ diversification is a stubborn baseline
\cite{demiguel2009naive}. We take the defensive posture these results imply: the
engine is deterministic and every figure is regenerated from it, we tune nothing
for performance, and we report a negative result (our weighting loses to equal
weighting and risk parity) rather than search for a configuration that wins.
This is the trustworthy-finance stance---explainable, reproducible, and
auditable decisions---applied to the unglamorous but universal cash-deployment
step.

\section{System Design}

The backend separates API DTOs, portfolio importers, provider adapters,
analytics scoring, and deployment planning. Network access is isolated behind
provider abstractions. The scoring and deployment modules perform no I/O: a
recommendation is a pure function of the tuple
\[
  (\text{holdings},\ \text{fundamentals},\ \text{cash},\ \text{policy version}).
\]
Referential transparency is what makes the rest of this paper possible. Every
figure and table below is produced by a single offline driver
(\texttt{scripts/run\_experiments.py}) and is regression-tested; no result
depends on live data or hidden state. The purity is enforced, not merely
asserted: a replay test feeds the same input tuple twice and checks that the
orders, exclusions, and ranked candidates are byte-identical. This is the
auditability guarantee a compliance reviewer needs---any past recommendation can
be regenerated and diffed from its logged inputs. Determinism is engineered, not
incidental: the engine draws no random numbers, ranking ties are broken by ticker
so the order does not depend on input ordering, and the scoring and sizing
arithmetic uses ordinary IEEE-754 doubles whose operations are reproducible
across platforms for these magnitudes.

The composite score is a linear functional of three bounded sub-scores,
\begin{equation}
  s = w_m\,\mathrm{moat} + w_c\,\mathrm{comp} + w_v\,\mathrm{val},
  \qquad \mathrm{moat},\mathrm{comp},\mathrm{val}\in[0,100],
  \label{eq:composite}
\end{equation}
with $(w_m,w_c,w_v)=(0.40,0.35,0.25)$. Holdings already above $1.3\times$ equal
weight, or in a theme at its concentration cap, are excluded; the planner then
ranks the remainder by $s$ and sizes orders against available cash.

\section{The Audit Protocol}
\label{sec:audit}

We evaluate an advisor not by how well it agrees with the baseline but by three
axes a single number conflates. Fix a \emph{scenario}: current holdings,
available cash, an allowed universe, per-scenario constraints (a recommendation
cap, add-only or trim rules, the economic order floor of
Section~\ref{sec:guardrail}), and the baseline's own recommendation. An advisor
returns a set of tickers (optionally with dollar amounts and cited facts). We
score it on:

\begin{description}
  \item[Validity.] The fraction of runs with no constraint violation:
  recommending outside the universe, exceeding the cap, buying an already-owned
  name under add-only, exceeding cash, ordering below the economic floor, or
  splitting cash so that the fee exceeds the single-order fee
  (Section~\ref{sec:fees}). Citing a fact absent from the scenario snapshot is
  also a violation, giving a check on explanation faithfulness.
  \item[Stability.] The mean pairwise Jaccard overlap of the recommended sets
  across repeated runs of the same scenario. A trustworthy advisor should not
  change its mind when nothing changed.
  \item[Agreement.] Overlap at $k$ and Jaccard against the baseline's ranking.
  This is reported, not optimized: disagreement may be useful novelty or a
  mistake, which only validity and realized outcome can disambiguate.
\end{description}

\paragraph{The axes are independent.}
To show that no single axis substitutes for the others, we run the protocol on
seven deterministic \emph{archetype} controls across frozen scenarios---failure
modes by construction, not model outputs
(Figure~\ref{fig:audit}). Two are decisive. A \emph{naive diversifier} that
agrees perfectly with the baseline ($\mathrm{agree}=1.0$) and never wavers
($\mathrm{stab}=1.0$) is nonetheless \emph{invalid} ($\mathrm{valid}=0.0$): it
splits sub-tranche cash and pays an avoidable fee. Conversely a
\emph{valid-but-disagreeing} control is fully valid and stable yet shares
nothing with the baseline ($\mathrm{agree}=0.0$). An evaluation reporting only
agreement would rank the first as ideal and the second as worst---exactly
inverting their operational validity. Validity, stability, and agreement are
therefore independent axes; an advisor benchmark must report all three.

\paragraph{Metrics, precisely.}
For an advisor that returns sets of tickers $r_1,\dots,r_n$ over $n$ repeated
runs of a scenario with baseline top-$k$ set $b$, we report
\[
  V = \tfrac1n\!\sum_j \mathbf{1}[\,\mathrm{viol}(r_j)=\varnothing\,],\quad
  S = \binom{n}{2}^{-1}\!\!\sum_{i<j}\! J(r_i,r_j),\quad
  A = \tfrac1n\!\sum_j \tfrac{|r_j\cap b|}{k},
\]
where $J$ is the Jaccard overlap and $\mathrm{viol}$ the constraint set of
Section~\ref{sec:audit}. Validity $V$ and stability $S$ are intrinsic to the
advisor; agreement $A$ is relative to the baseline and reported, never optimized.
All three are deterministic given the runs, so the audit of a fixed set of
outputs is itself replayable.

\paragraph{What the protocol does and does not certify.}
It certifies \emph{process}---that advice is admissible, repeatable, and grounded
in the scenario---not \emph{outcome}. A valid, stable, agreeing advisor can still
be wrong if the baseline itself is wrong; this is exactly why we calibrate the
baseline against standard allocators and report that it does not beat them
(Section~\ref{sec:weighting}). Agreement is descriptive: disagreement may be
useful novelty or error, separable only by realized outcome. Stability is
measured over ticker sets, so an advisor consistent in names but erratic in
sizing would need an amount-aware extension. The archetypes are controls that
establish discriminating power, not claims about any model. These boundaries are
what keep the protocol honest: it tells a practitioner whether an advisor is
\emph{safe to deploy}, a prerequisite that return curves alone never establish.

The archetypes establish the protocol and its discriminating power offline and
deterministically. Running real language-model advisors through the same frozen
scenarios is the natural next study; the protocol, scenarios, and metrics are
already in place to receive their outputs without changing the rules.

\section{The Fee Structure as an Object of Study}
\label{sec:fees}

The broker charges a fixed cost $c$ per \emph{started} tranche of size $T$ in a
single-symbol order. A partially used tranche is billed in full, so the fee is
the \emph{ceiling} (not the floor) of the tranche count:
\begin{equation}
  f(a) = \left\lceil \tfrac{a}{T} \right\rceil c,
  \qquad T = \$1000,\ c = \$2.50.
  \label{eq:fee}
\end{equation}
For example $f(\$1{,}001)=\lceil 1.001\rceil c = 2c = \$5.00$, the same as
$f(\$2{,}000)$; a single dollar past a boundary starts a new tranche. The fee is
charged per order for one symbol, so deploying cash across $k$ distinct symbols
incurs $k$ separate fees. We assume fractional shares, so order size is
continuous in dollars; integer-share rounding would only add a second, smaller
quantization on top of the tranche effect and does not change the argument.

\begin{proposition}[Subadditivity]
\label{prop:sub}
$f$ is subadditive: for all $a,b \ge 0$, $f(a+b) \le f(a) + f(b)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The ceiling is subadditive, $\lceil x+y\rceil \le \lceil x\rceil + \lceil y\rceil$
for all reals $x,y$, because $\lceil x\rceil + \lceil y\rceil$ is an integer that
is $\ge x+y$ and the ceiling is the least such integer. Setting $x=a/T$, $y=b/T$
and multiplying by $c>0$ gives the claim. (The floor is \emph{super}additive, so
a floor-based fee would not yield this inequality; the ``per started tranche''
billing is what makes the cost subadditive.)
\end{proof}

The consequence is immediate and practical: \emph{consolidating cash into one
single-symbol order weakly minimizes fees}. Splitting a fixed budget across
orders $a_1,\dots,a_k$ (necessarily across distinct symbols) pays a
\emph{diversification fee premium}
\begin{equation}
  \pi(a_1,\dots,a_k) = \sum_i f(a_i) - f\!\Big(\sum_i a_i\Big) \;\ge\; 0,
  \label{eq:premium}
\end{equation}
where the single-order reference $f(\sum_i a_i)$ is the hypothetical cost of the
same dollars in one symbol; it is a lower bound on any multi-symbol split, not an
achievable diversified portfolio. The premium quantifies what diversification
costs under this fee, and motivates the fee-aware planner below.

We swept cash from \$250 to \$5000 and compared three policies against the
single-order lower bound: a \emph{naive} split strictly proportional to score, a
\emph{fee-aware} planner, and (as a reference) the single order. Naive splitting
incurs $\pi=\$2.50$ in recurring bands wherever the two legs cross an extra
tranche boundary (Figure~\ref{fig:premium}).

\paragraph{A defect surfaced by the sweep.}
The sweep did not merely confirm theory; it falsified an assumption in our own
code. The planner's prior implementation aligned one leg to a whole tranche to
preserve the single-order fee, which is correct for cash above one tranche but
\emph{not} for sub-tranche cash. The exact failure region has a closed form.
Write $\mathrm{MIN}$ for the economic order floor derived in
Section~\ref{sec:guardrail}, and let a budget $a$ be split by score share into
legs $a\rho$ and $a(1-\rho)$ with the larger share $\rho\in[\tfrac12,1)$.

\begin{proposition}[Sub-tranche fee-worsening]
\label{prop:worsen}
If both legs clear the floor and the budget fits one tranche, i.e.
\[
  \frac{\mathrm{MIN}}{1-\rho} \;\le\; a \;\le\; T,
\]
then the two-order split overpays consolidation by exactly one unit of cost:
$f(a\rho)+f(a(1-\rho))-f(a)=c$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The lower bound gives $a(1-\rho)\ge\mathrm{MIN}>0$, and $\rho\ge\tfrac12$ gives
$a\rho\ge a(1-\rho)>0$, so both legs are positive and each at most $a\le T$; hence
$\lceil a\rho/T\rceil=\lceil a(1-\rho)/T\rceil=1$. The budget $a\le T$ gives
$\lceil a/T\rceil=1$. The difference is $c(1+1)-c(1)=c$.
\end{proof}

With $\mathrm{MIN}=\$250$, $\rho=0.6$, $T=\$1000$ the band is $[\$625,\$1000]$,
exactly the region the sweep flagged. The general fix follows from
Proposition~\ref{prop:sub}: diversify \emph{only when it is fee-neutral}, i.e.
keep the split iff $f(a\rho)+f(a(1-\rho))=f(a)$, otherwise consolidate. A property
test asserts $\,\text{planner fee}\le f(\text{cash})\,$ across the grid, and the
engine premium is now zero everywhere it deploys (Figure~\ref{fig:premium},
green).

The two-order rule extends to any number of legs, which bounds how widely a
budget can be diversified without paying for it.

\begin{proposition}[$k$-way fee-neutrality]
\label{prop:kway}
A split of budget $a$ into legs $a_1,\dots,a_k$ ($\sum_i a_i=a$, $a_i>0$) is
fee-neutral, $\sum_i f(a_i)=f(a)$, iff
$\sum_i \lceil a_i/T\rceil = \lceil a/T\rceil$. The number of fee-neutral legs is
therefore at most $\lceil a/T\rceil$, and this bound is achieved by giving each
leg a full tranche. Requiring each leg also to be economic ($a_i\ge\mathrm{MIN}$)
caps the breadth at $\min\!\big(\lceil a/T\rceil, \lfloor a/\mathrm{MIN}\rfloor\big)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$f(a_i)=\lceil a_i/T\rceil c$ and $f(a)=\lceil a/T\rceil c$, so neutrality is
exactly $\sum_i\lceil a_i/T\rceil=\lceil a/T\rceil$. Each leg contributes
$\lceil a_i/T\rceil\ge 1$, so $k\le\sum_i\lceil a_i/T\rceil=\lceil a/T\rceil$;
$k=\lceil a/T\rceil$ is reachable by allocating whole tranches. The economic
constraint $a_i\ge\mathrm{MIN}$ admits at most $\lfloor a/\mathrm{MIN}\rfloor$
legs, giving the stated minimum.
\end{proof}

This unifies the two guardrails: fee-neutrality limits diversification breadth to
$\lceil a/T\rceil$ tranches, and the economic floor limits it to
$\lfloor a/\mathrm{MIN}\rfloor$ orders; the planner funds the smaller of the two.
For small incremental cash both bounds are one or two, which is why the planner
rarely places more than a pair of orders.

\section{The Guardrail Is a Fixed Point}
\label{sec:guardrail}

A trade should not lose a meaningful fraction of its value to fees. Fixing a
tolerance $\tau$ on fee impact for a single-tranche order gives
$f_{\min}/a \le \tau$, i.e. $c/a \le \tau$. The binding case yields the economic
order floor
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MIN} = \frac{c}{\tau} = \frac{\$2.50}{0.01} = \$250,
  \label{eq:min}
\end{equation}
the cash level at which one tranche's fee is exactly $\tau=1\%$ of the order.
This is a \emph{derived fixed point of the cost model}, not a tuned parameter.
Below \$250 the planner holds cash; a second economic order requires
$2\,\mathrm{MIN}=\$500$. Figure~\ref{fig:guardrail} shows fee impact touching
exactly $1\%$ at the floor and declining thereafter, with sawtooths only at the
genuine tranche boundaries.

The closed form $\mathrm{MIN}=c/\tau$ holds only while it stays inside the first
tranche. The next proposition makes the regime explicit and shows what happens
outside it.

\begin{proposition}[Feasibility of the floor]
\label{prop:floor}
Let $g(a)=f(a)/a=\lceil a/T\rceil c/a$ be the fee impact of a single order of
size $a$. Within tranche $k$ (for $a\in((k-1)T, kT]$) $g$ decreases from near
$kc/((k-1)T)$ to its tranche minimum $g(kT)=c/T$; hence $\min_{a>0} g(a)=c/T$,
attained at every boundary $a=kT$. Consequently, for a tolerance $\tau$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item if $\tau \ge c/T$, the smallest order meeting the tolerance is
  $\mathrm{MIN}=c/\tau\le T$ (first tranche), the fixed point of
  Eq.~(\ref{eq:min});
  \item if $\tau < c/T$, no order meets the tolerance: $g(a)>\tau$ for all
  $a>0$, and in particular the naive value $a=c/\tau>T$ pays
  $\lceil a/T\rceil\,c/a \ge 2\tau$.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
On tranche $k$, $\lceil a/T\rceil=k$ is constant, so $g(a)=kc/a$ is strictly
decreasing; its infimum on the tranche is at the right endpoint $a=kT$, where
$g=kc/(kT)=c/T$, independent of $k$. Thus the global minimum of $g$ is $c/T$. If
$\tau\ge c/T$ the equation $c/a=\tau$ has its solution $a=c/\tau$ in $(0,T]$, and
$g$ first reaches $\tau$ there; if $\tau<c/T$ then $g(a)\ge c/T>\tau$ everywhere.
For $a=c/\tau>T$ we have $\lceil a/T\rceil\ge 2$, so $g(a)\ge 2c/a=2\tau$.
\end{proof}

With $c=\$2.50$ and $T=\$1000$ the feasibility threshold is $c/T=0.25\%$, so the
$\$250$ floor is well inside the valid regime at $\tau=1\%$. The proposition also
says the cost model has a hard floor on fee impact, $c/T$: no order, however
large within a tranche, is cheaper than a quarter percent except exactly at a
tranche boundary.

\paragraph{Sensitivity and generality.}
The \$250 number is specific to our $(c,\tau)$; the formula is not. Since
$\mathrm{MIN}=c/\tau$, the floor is linear in the fixed cost, inverse in the
tolerance, and \emph{independent of the tranche size} $T$: any positive order
starts exactly one tranche, so the binding fee at the floor is one unit of $c$
regardless of $T$ (which only rescales the premium bands of
Section~\ref{sec:fees}). Proposition~\ref{prop:floor} gives the floor for any
schedule with an additive fixed cost. At $\tau=1\%$ a flat \$1 fee yields a \$100
floor, \$5 yields \$500, and \$9.99 yields \$999; the guardrail degenerates to
``always trade'' under zero commission ($c=0$). The studied \$2.50 tranche (floor
\$250) is typical for brokered international or non-US-equity access.

\section{What Actually Drives the Decision}
\label{sec:weighting}

Because the composite (\ref{eq:composite}) is linear, the ranking it induces is
directly ablatable: zeroing a weight isolates a factor, and the score can be
recomposed from per-position sub-scores without re-fetching data.

\paragraph{Ablation (mechanism).}
On a fixed, deterministic basket we recomposed the ranking under single-factor
and equal weights and measured Spearman correlation to the baseline ordering and
the churn in the funded top-2 (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}). Single factors reorder the basket sharply---moat-only
and valuation-only are \emph{anti-correlated} with the baseline---while equal
thirds reproduce the baseline almost exactly ($\rho=0.97$, zero churn). The
funded picks are stable under $\pm10\%$ weight perturbation. (This basket uses
deterministic synthetic fundamentals; it demonstrates the \emph{mechanism}, not a
claim about specific securities.)

\paragraph{Backtest against allocator baselines (real data).}
We backtested the 17-name basket against four baselines over 2021-01-05 to
2026-05-28 (1355 trading days) using free adjusted closes
(Table~\ref{tab:backtest}). Two are naive
(market-value ``current'' weighting and equal weighting); two are allocator
baselines computable from the return matrix alone---long-only minimum variance
and equal-risk-contribution (risk parity)---so no baseline uses information the
others lack. Both allocators estimate the sample covariance over the full window
with no shrinkage, are long-only and fully invested ($w\ge 0$, $\mathbf{1}^\top
w=1$), and set weights once at the start (buy and hold, no rebalancing), so they
are compared on the same footing as the other buy-and-hold baselines; minimum
variance uses an active-set reduction on the closed-form solution and risk parity
the Maillard fixed-point iteration. Reported estimation choices are deliberately
plain, since the point is a fair reference, not a tuned competitor. The
score-weighted basket beats the SPY benchmark by a wide margin
($+4.80$ pp CAGR, $+0.15$ Sharpe) and beats minimum variance on return
($+3.8$ pp CAGR), but it ranks \emph{third of five} on the Sharpe ratio: equal
weighting ($1.19$) and risk parity ($1.18$) both edge out the tuned weighting
($1.10$), and minimum variance delivers the shallowest drawdown ($-19.2\%$ versus
$-28.9\%$) at the cost of return. Periodic rebalancing \emph{reduced} CAGR to
$18.5\%$ from the $20.6\%$ buy-and-hold figure despite low explicit cost. The
synthetic ablation and the real backtest point the same way: on this basket, two
allocators that use \emph{no} fundamental data match or beat the
fundamentals-weighted strategy on risk-adjusted return.

\paragraph{Is the result robust?}
A single full-sample comparison cannot separate structure from luck, so we probe
the equal-versus-current result two ways over the same daily-return matrix, using
fixed-weight (daily-rebalanced) portfolios for a clean Sharpe comparison. First,
rolling one-year windows stepped monthly: equal weighting has the higher Sharpe in
$47$ of $53$ windows ($89\%$; Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}), failing only in a
single 2024--2025 stretch. Second, a circular block bootstrap ($2{,}000$ resamples
of 21-day blocks, fixed seed) on the full-period Sharpe difference
$\mathrm{Sharpe}(\text{equal})-\mathrm{Sharpe}(\text{current})$ gives a point
estimate of $+0.076$ with a $95\%$ interval $[-0.008, +0.168]$ and
$P(\text{equal better})=96\%$. The direction is robust; the magnitude is
\emph{not} decisive at the $5\%$ level, the interval grazing zero. We therefore
state the finding precisely: on this basket equal weighting is reliably at least
as good as the tuned weighting, which is enough to support the methodological
claim without overstating performance.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Principal contribution.}
The contribution is a way to evaluate investment advice: an audit protocol that
scores any advisor on validity, stability, and agreement against a deterministic,
replayable baseline, and the finding that these axes are independent so an
agreement- or return-only score is misleading (Section~\ref{sec:audit}). The
baseline earns the role of yardstick by being provably reproducible---its fee is
subadditive and its order floor is a closed-form fixed point
(Equations~(\ref{eq:fee})--(\ref{eq:min}))---not by claiming to beat the market.
This is a performance-free, falsifiable basis for asking when learned advice
actually helps.

\paragraph{Takeaways and a useful negative result.}
First, in fee-quantized markets, diversification is not free; the right default
is to diversify only when fee-neutral. Second, the cost guardrail is derivable,
not tunable. Third---the result we did not expect---the fundamentals-weighted
basket ranks third of five on risk-adjusted return, behind both equal weighting
and risk parity, two allocators that consume no fundamental data. This is
consistent with $1/N$ diversification being hard to beat \cite{demiguel2009naive}.
Selection appears to matter more than weighting here; we would rather report this
than hide it. The result also clarifies where the contribution does \emph{not}
lie: not in outperforming standard allocators, but in making each deployment
decision cheap, bounded, and auditable.

\paragraph{Serendipity of measurement.}
Each finding came from instrumenting a deterministic engine rather than from a
prior hypothesis. The fee defect was invisible in code review and obvious in a
parameter sweep. The audit's central finding was the same kind of surprise: a
control built only to ``agree with the policy'' scored a perfect agreement and
stability yet was invalid, which is what convinced us that agreement-only
evaluation---common in LLM-advice studies---hides operational failure.
Reproducibility was not a compliance checkbox but the instrument that produced
the science.

\section{Limitations}

The audit protocol is demonstrated on deterministic archetype controls, not on
live language-model outputs; the controls establish that the three axes
discriminate, but a study of real advisors is required to draw conclusions about
any specific model, and is the immediate next step. The backtest is a
current-basket study on free adjusted closes; it is subject to survivorship and
selection bias and is \emph{not} a point-in-time stock-selection backtest. The
ablation uses deterministic synthetic fundamentals and demonstrates mechanism,
not security-level claims. The cost model is the
fixed per-order tranche fee only: it omits bid-ask spreads, slippage,
integer-lot constraints, and taxes, and it applies where brokers charge such a
fee (common for international or non-US-equity access) rather than under
zero-commission US-equity trading. The guardrail generalizes to any fee with a
fixed per-order component, but the specific \$250 floor is a function of the
$(c,\tau)$ pair. We probe the weighting result with rolling windows and a block
bootstrap, but on a single basket; out-of-sample point-in-time evaluation, richer
cost models, and \emph{cross-universe} robustness over independent baskets remain
future work. The system is not a registered
investment adviser, broker-dealer, or execution system; commercial use requires
legal, compliance, data-licensing, and suitability review.

\section{The Audit Protocol's Decision Space}
\label{sec:decision-space}

The independence of the three axes (valid, agree, stab) creates an operational
decision space where an AI advisor's output can be characterized precisely. Each
axis is measured over frozen scenarios with deterministic controls, producing a
point $(v,a,s)\in[0,1]^3$.

The discovery that independence holds has two implications. First, practitioners
and regulators can no longer rely on a single metric (e.g., agreement via overlap@k,
which several LLM-advice papers report). Second, the protocol can be calibrated
around a safety threshold---e.g., $v > \tau_v$ (regulatory guardrail), $s >
\tau_s$ (operational stability floor)---which a real advisor either crosses or does
not. This turns evaluation from a ranking into a compliance gate.

The existing baseline (deterministic policy) occupies $(1,1,1)$, the corner of
validity, perfect agreement, and stability. The archetype controls form a
polytope around it: naive diversifier at $(0,1,1)$, valid-but-disagreeing at
$(1,0,1)$, and drifting advisor at $(1,0.4,0.07)$. Real advisors will land
inside or outside this convex hull. A venue focused on LLM robustness should
measure all three; a compliance regime should set thresholds and audit membership.
The auditor is thus simultaneously a research tool (showing what can fail in each
dimension) and a deployment gate (showing what threshold an advisor must clear).

\section{Conclusion}

As language models enter investment advice, the field needs a way to ask whether
their advice is trustworthy that does not reduce to a noisy return or a
misleading agreement score. We offered one: a deterministic, replayable baseline
whose cost behavior is provable, and an audit protocol that scores any advisor on
validity, stability, and agreement at once. The protocol's own finding---that
these axes are independent, so agreement alone certifies nothing---is the kind of
hidden failure a benchmark exists to expose. The baseline is deliberately
self-critical: equal weighting matches it, so it makes no performance claim and
serves purely as the transparent reference a learned advisor must be measured
against. Dropping real models into the frozen scenarios is the natural next study,
and the artifact is built to receive them without changing the rules.
